The idea

A good manager is one whose teams keep beating the prediction

Team results mix the manager's contribution with the skills of the people they happened to get. The studies separate the two the way a causal analysis would. They predict what each team should score from its members' individually tested skills, which is the counterfactual of an average manager, and then measure how far each manager's teams land above or below that prediction, across several randomly assigned teams.

The procedure

  1. Test everyone on their own first

    Measure task-specific skill with an individual version of the team task (Study 1: numerical, spatial and analytical problems; Study 2: a solo hidden-profile puzzle). Add fluid intelligence and any other candidate predictors you want to check later.

  2. Rotate managers through random teams

    Each manager leads several teams of randomly drawn members (4 in Study 1, 6 in Study 2), with as few repeat pairings as possible. Random assignment makes the manager effect causal: nobody gets to pick their team.

  3. Predict every team's score from skills alone

    Standardise team scores within each round, then regress them on the manager's and the members' task skill.

    Gg = βM·skillmanager + βW·skillmembers + εg

  4. Average each manager's surplus

    A manager's effect is the mean residual of the teams they led. A multilevel model separates real differences between managers (σα) from noise, and randomization inference checks whether the spread exceeds what reshuffled assignments produce.

    α̂i = mean(ε̂g for teams led by i); ε̂gi = αi + egi, αi ~ N(0, σα²)

  5. Optional: replace the human followers with AI agents

    In Study 2, leaders also directed teams of LLM agents through the same kind of puzzle. Their AI-team scores tracked their causal effect on human teams closely, at a fraction of the cost and without coordinating groups of people.

Study 1 · NBER w32699 · 2024

How Do You Find a Good Manager?

Weidmann, Vecci, Said, Deming & Bhalotra. 555 participants, 728 three-person teams, Collaborative Production Task, 4 random rotations, pre-registered.

0.23 SD

Typical manager effect. A manager 1 SD above average raises team performance by 0.23 SD (randomization inference p = .04), about as much as raising both workers' production skill by 1 SD (0.26 SD). A good manager is worth almost twice a good worker.

−0.10

Self-nominated managers did worse than managers picked by lottery (−0.10 to −0.13 SD, marginally significant). Self-promoters were more overconfident, especially about their social skills.

r .24

Economic decision-making and fluid IQ predicted manager performance (lottery arm). Gender, age, ethnicity and personality did not.

8% vs 16%

How good managers help: they monitor (half the wasted effort), assign people by comparative advantage, and keep teams motivated late in the task.

Study 2 · NBER w33662 · 2025

Measuring Human Leadership Skills with AI Agents

Weidmann, Xu & Deming. 249 leaders, hidden-profile puzzles, 6 human teams and 6 AI teams (GPT-4o followers) per leader, order counterbalanced, pre-registered.

ρ .81

AI-team scores matched human ground truth. Disattenuated correlation 0.81 (raw 0.67); 0.69 after controlling for hard skills.

0.55 SD

Leader effect beyond hard skills, nearly identical in the AI and human versions (about 0.65 SD before skill controls).

Asks

Good leaders asked more questions and had more back-and-forth. Talking more did not help. Positive tone mattered only with human followers.

$23

Per participant for the AI test, versus $114 and two supervising researchers for the human version.

Read the results with care

  • Both papers are working papers, circulated for discussion and not yet peer reviewed. The tasks are lab tasks done mostly by students; field validation is still to come.
  • The causal reading depends on random assignment. In everyday HR data, managers and teams are not matched at random, so the same calculation would be confounded.
  • With four or six rotations, the estimate for any single manager is noisy. Study 1 therefore focused on the typical size of manager effects (σα) and on what predicts them. Use the reliability calculator before ranking individuals.
  • Treat a score as one input among several, and check for adverse impact before using any test in selection.

Running it well

  • Use a task with an objective score that needs real collaboration and gives the manager real work: assigning people, monitoring progress, keeping the team going. Study 1 scored teams on their weakest module, so nobody could carry the team alone.
  • Measure individual skill before the team sessions, with an individual analogue of the team task. That measure is what lets the analysis separate leadership from the luck of the draw.
  • Keep roles fixed and assignment random. Use a fresh, parallel version of the task in each round. The analysis standardises scores within rounds, so differences in difficulty between rounds wash out.
  • Record the selection route (self-nominated, appointed, lottery) if you want to compare them, and collect candidate predictors such as decision-making tests or 360 ratings for the correlation analysis.
Step 2

Estimate manager effects

Load one row per team per round, plus an optional file of individual skill scores. The analysis follows the pre-registered models of Study 1: conditioning regression, manager averages, a REML multilevel model with profile-likelihood intervals, randomization inference, leave-one-round-out validation and the correlates of good management.
